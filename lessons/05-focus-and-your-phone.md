# Lesson 5: Focus and your phone
<!-- facts: Focus, phones and breaks -->

## Check in

Ask whether they took their tiny first step, and how it felt before and after. Was the task as bad
as they expected?

## By the end, the learner can

- Explain why switching between tasks, and notifications, cost focus.
- Set up their phone to interrupt them less, using its built-in settings.
- Use timed work and breaks, and say honestly how strong the evidence is.

## Key ideas

**Multitasking is switching.** You can't really do two thinking tasks at once; you switch between
them, and each switch costs a little time. The costs add up, especially with harder tasks
(`facts.md`).

**Notifications pull you away even if you ignore them.** In one study, just getting a call or text
alert, without answering it, hurt attention about as much as using the phone (`facts.md`).

**What's less clear.** A famous study said a phone merely on the table drains your brain. A
careful repeat found no such effect, so it's unproven (`facts.md`). What matters most is what the
phone does (buzzing, lighting up), not that it's there.

**Free fixes on any phone:**
- Turn off notifications for apps that aren't people or urgent: games, shopping, most social media.
- Use the built-in focus or do-not-disturb mode, allowing calls from the few people who may need
  you, such as a child's school or the person you care for.
- Move tempting apps off the home screen.
- Check messages at set times instead of all day.

If you use your phone for work, or someone depends on reaching you, keep that channel open. The
aim is fewer pointless interruptions, not being unreachable.

**Timed work and breaks.** The Pomodoro idea is to work for a set time, then take a short break.
One small study found students on fixed breaks felt less tired and distracted than students who
chose their own breaks (`facts.md`). It's promising, not proven; the exact minutes matter less
than stopping before you're drained.

**Short bursts suit busy lives.** If you only get 15 minutes between chores or on a commute, one
focused block with notifications off beats an hour of half-attention.

## Worked example

Ayesha studies at night after her children sleep, but keeps checking her phone. She turns off
social media notifications, sets do-not-disturb to let her mother's calls through, and works in
blocks: 25 minutes reading, 5 minutes break. She finishes a chapter that used to take all evening.

## Exercise

Ask the learner to guess how many notifications they get a day, then check their phone's settings
(many phones show it) and pick three apps to silence.

## Task for next time

Silence at least three apps, and try one timed focus block (any length) on something important.
The tutor will ask what changed.
