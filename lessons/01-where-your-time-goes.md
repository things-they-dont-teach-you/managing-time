# Lesson 1: Where your time really goes
<!-- facts: Time management research; Planning fallacy -->

## By the end, the learner can

- Explain why managing time is as much about feeling less stretched as about getting more done.
- Run a simple time audit for a few days, using only a phone or paper.
- Spot their biggest fixed blocks (work, commute, care, sleep) and their leaks.
- Explain the planning fallacy, and why their own estimates run short.

## Key ideas

**Why bother.** The biggest review of time management research found it is linked to wellbeing at
least as strongly as to performance (`facts.md`). The aim isn't to squeeze more in; it's to feel
more in control of the hours you have.

**You can't manage what you haven't seen.** Most people guess badly where their time goes. A
**time audit** is just writing it down for a few days, as it happens, not from memory at night.

**How to do it on a phone.** Open your notes app. Every time you switch activity, or every hour or
two, write the time and a few words: "06:10 taxi to work", "13:00 lunch, scrolled", "19:30 kids'
homework". Paper works just as well. Don't try to change anything yet; just look.

**Three kinds of time** show up in most audits:
- **Fixed:** shifts, commuting, school runs, caring, sleep. Hard to move.
- **Flexible:** free time you choose how to use.
- **Leaks:** time that slips away without you choosing it, such as waiting, scrolling, or tasks
  that took twice as long as planned.

**Commutes and waiting are not empty.** Twenty minutes in a queue or a taxi can hold low-focus
tasks: a reminder list, a voice note, a podcast lesson, planning tomorrow.

**The planning fallacy.** People underestimate how long their own tasks take, even when they know
similar tasks ran late before (`facts.md`). Your audit shows how long things really take, which is
the best cure.

## Worked example

Nomsa works shifts at a supermarket and looks after her younger brother after school. She thinks
she has "no time at all". Her three-day audit shows about two and a half hours a day in taxis and
queues, a fixed four-hour block for her brother, and about an hour a day that slips into scrolling
before bed. The commute becomes her planning time; the hour before bed is the one she chooses to
win back, a bit at a time.

## Exercise

Ask the learner to describe yesterday, hour by hour, as best they can remember. Then ask which
parts were fixed, which flexible, and where they think time leaked.

## Task for next time

Keep a time audit for at least two days (one work or busy day, one other), in a notes app or on
paper. Next session, the tutor will ask what surprised them. They don't need to share the whole
log.
