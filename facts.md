# Facts and figures

**Applies to: no expiry, but research moves on.** Re-check each source when it is more than a year
past its checked date.

Every finding below comes from the linked study or official page, on the date shown. Most are
studies of students or office workers in a few rich countries, so treat them as good evidence about
people in general, not a promise about you. Lessons point here instead of repeating numbers, so
updating this file updates the course.

## Time management research

Source: [Aeon, Faber and Panaccio (2021), Does time management work? A meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0245066), PLOS ONE, checked 2026-11-11.

| Finding | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Size of the review | 158 studies, more than 53 000 people, six continents |
| Time management and wellbeing | Moderately linked; the link with wellbeing is at least as strong as with job performance |
| Time management and job or school results | Moderately linked; weaker for school results, no clear link with test scores |
| Caveat | Most studies measure links at one point in time, not cause and effect |

## Planning fallacy

Source: [Buehler, Griffin and Ross (1994), Exploring the "planning fallacy"](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.67.3.366), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, checked 2026-11-11.

People consistently underestimate how long their own tasks will take, even when they know similar
tasks ran late before. They focus on the plan and forget past delays and interruptions.

## Urgency and importance

Source: [Zhu, Yang and Hsee (2018), The mere urgency effect](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucy008), Journal of Consumer Research, checked 2026-11-11.

- In experiments, people chose urgent tasks (short deadlines) over important ones (bigger payoff),
  even when the important task was plainly the better choice.
- The effect was stronger in people who see themselves as busy.
- Reminding people of each task's payoff at the moment of choosing made the effect smaller.

The urgent/important grid (often called the Eisenhower matrix) is a planning tool built on this
idea. The grid itself has not been tested as a method; the bias it guards against has.

## If-then plans

Sources, checked 2026-11-11:
[Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006), Implementation intentions and goal achievement](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1),
Advances in Experimental Social Psychology;
[Sheeran, Listrom and Gollwitzer (2024), The when and how of planning](https://doi.org/10.1080/10463283.2024.2334563),
European Review of Social Psychology.

| Finding | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| What it is | A plan in the form "If (situation), then I will (action)" |
| 2006 review | 94 tests, more than 8 000 people: a medium-to-large effect on reaching goals (d = 0.65) |
| 2024 review | 642 tests: helped across thinking, feeling and behaviour outcomes (d from 0.27 to 0.66) |
| Caveat | Later, bias-corrected estimates are smaller than the 2006 figure; it helps, but less than first thought |

## Habits

Source: [Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts and Wardle (2010), How are habits formed](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674), European Journal of Social Psychology, checked 2026-11-11.

| Finding | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Time for a daily habit to feel automatic | Median 66 days, range 18 to 254 days |
| Missing one day | Did not noticeably harm habit forming |
| Caveat | Small study (the 66 days comes from 39 people), partly extrapolated |

The popular "21 days to form a habit" has no research behind it.

## Procrastination

Sources, checked 2026-11-11:
[Steel (2007), The nature of procrastination](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65), Psychological Bulletin;
[Sirois and Pychyl (2013), Procrastination and the priority of short-term mood regulation](https://doi.org/10.1111/spc3.12011), Social and Personality Psychology Compass;
[Wohl, Pychyl and Bennett (2010), I forgive myself, now I can study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2010.01.029), Personality and Individual Differences;
[van Eerde and Klingsieck (2018), Overcoming procrastination? A meta-analysis of intervention studies](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2018.09.002), Educational Research Review.

| Finding | Source |
| --- | --- |
| Tasks that feel unpleasant, and rewards that are far away, are put off most; impulsive people procrastinate more | Steel 2007 |
| Procrastination works as short-term mood repair: avoiding the task feels better now, at a cost later | Sirois and Pychyl 2013 |
| Students who forgave themselves for procrastinating before one exam procrastinated less before the next (heavy procrastinators only; self-reported) | Wohl and others 2010 |
| Across 24 intervention studies, procrastination dropped and stayed lower; cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) helped most | van Eerde and Klingsieck 2018 |

## Focus, phones and breaks

Sources, checked 2026-11-11:
[American Psychological Association, Multitasking: switching costs](https://www.apa.org/topics/research/multitasking);
[Stothart, Mitchum and Yehnert (2015), The attentional cost of receiving a cell phone notification](https://doi.org/10.1037/xhp0000100), Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance;
[Ward and others (2017), Brain drain](https://doi.org/10.1086/691462), Journal of the Association for Consumer Research;
[Ruiz Pardo and Minda (2022), Reexamining the "brain drain" effect](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.actpsy.2022.103717), Acta Psychologica;
[Biwer and others (2023), Comparing "Pomodoro" breaks and self-regulated breaks](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjep.12593), British Journal of Educational Psychology.

| Finding | Source |
| --- | --- |
| Switching between tasks costs time; each switch is small (fractions of a second) but they add up, more so with complex or unfamiliar tasks | APA |
| Just receiving a call or text notification, without answering it, hurt performance on an attention task about as much as using the phone | Stothart and others 2015 |
| A 2017 study found that a phone merely nearby reduced working memory | Ward and others 2017 |
| A careful replication found no such effect, so this is **unproven** | Ruiz Pardo and Minda 2022 |
| Students on fixed breaks (24 minutes work, 6 minutes break) reported less tiredness and distraction than students who chose their own breaks, and finished similar work in less time | Biwer and others 2023 (87 students, one university) |

## Sleep and shift work

Sources, checked 2026-11-11:
[CDC, FastStats: Sleep in adults](https://www.cdc.gov/sleep/data-and-statistics/adults.html);
[NIOSH, Training for nurses on shift work and long work hours](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/bulletin/2015/nurses-shiftwork.html).

| Finding | Detail |
| --- | --- |
| Recommended sleep for adults | At least 7 hours a day (CDC) |
| Being awake 17 hours | Performance similar to a blood alcohol level of 0.05% (NIOSH) |
| Being awake 24 hours | Similar to 0.10% (NIOSH) |

The NIOSH course is written for nurses but its sleep and coping modules apply to any shift worker.

## Burnout

Source: [World Health Organization, Burn-out an "occupational phenomenon"](https://www.who.int/standards/classifications/frequently-asked-questions/burn-out-an-occupational-phenomenon), checked 2026-11-11.

WHO's ICD-11 describes burn-out as a syndrome from chronic workplace stress that has not been
successfully managed, not as a medical condition. Three signs:

1. Feeling drained or exhausted.
2. Mental distance from your job, or negative, cynical feelings about it.
3. Feeling less effective at work.

WHO uses the term for work only, not other areas of life. Exhaustion from caring for family is
real, but it is not "burn-out" in WHO's sense.

## Unproven productivity claims

Checked 2026-11-11. These are common online but have no good research behind them, or the research
went against them. The course mentions them only to label them.

| Claim | What the evidence says |
| --- | --- |
| "It takes 21 days to form a habit" | No study behind it; see Habits |
| "Multitasking makes you more productive" | Switching costs time; see Focus, phones and breaks |
| "Just having your phone nearby drains your brain" | One study found it; replications did not |
| "Successful people all wake up at 5 a.m." | No good evidence that the hour matters more than enough sleep |
| "Do your hardest task first thing (eat the frog)" | A popular tip, not tested as a method; may suit some people |
